Ada Lovelace

Poet of numbers / First algorithm / Vision beyond the machine

Biography

Augusta Ada King, Countess of Lovelace (1815-1852), was an English mathematician and writer best known for her work on Charles Babbage's proposed mechanical general-purpose computer, the Analytical Engine. Raised by her mother to focus on rigorous mathematics - a deliberate counter to the "poetry mad" reputation of her father, Lord Byron - Ada blended analytical discipline with imaginative foresight.

In 1842, Italian engineer Luigi Menabrea published a paper on the Analytical Engine. Ada translated it and, at the request of Babbage, added her own extensive notes. Note G contained what is widely recognised as the first algorithm intended to be carried out by a machine: a step-by-step procedure for computing Bernoulli numbers.

Beyond the algorithm, Ada imagined machines that could manipulate symbols of any kind - not just numbers - and that music itself might one day be processed by such engines. That vision anticipated modern computing by more than a century.

Though the Analytical Engine was never built in her lifetime, Ada's writings established a conceptual foundation for software as distinct from hardware. Her legacy endures in the Ada programming language, NASA's Ada mission support systems, and every engineer who writes instructions rather than only circuits.

Key achievements

  1. 1815 Born 10 December in London, daughter of Lord Byron and Anne Isabella Milbanke.
  2. 1833 Introduced to Charles Babbage and his Difference Engine; begins scientific circles in London.
  3. 1835 Marrys William King; later Countess of Lovelace. Continues mathematical study with Augustus De Morgan.
  4. 1842-43 Translates Menabrea's paper and publishes Notes A-G, including the first published computer program (Note G).
  5. 1843 Predicts general-purpose computation: the Engine might compose music or manipulate any symbol.
  6. 1852 Dies 27 November at age 36; buried beside her mother at the Church of St. Mary Magdalene, Hucknall.
"The Analytical Engine weaves algebraic patterns just as the Jacquard loom weaves flowers and leaves." - Ada Lovelace, Notes on the Analytical Engine, 1843
